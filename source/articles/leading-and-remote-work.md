# Leading without authority and working asynchronously

**Covers:** Leading without authority, Async

## Getting things done through others {#overview}
Project managers often have a hard job. They are responsible for a result, but they cannot hire, fire or set pay for the people who produce it. On top of that, many teams are spread across cities and time zones, so people cannot always meet live. Both situations call for skills that depend on trust and clear writing more than on position.

## Leading without authority {#leading-without-authority}
How do you get a team to follow your direction when you have no formal power over them? This is normal in projects, where team members report to other managers and lend their time to you. We call it leading without authority.

Here is what works instead of power:
- **Clarity:** a clear goal and a clear reason. People follow a purpose better than an order.
- **Trust:** keep small promises, admit mistakes and give credit.
- **Reciprocity:** help others with their problems, and they will help with yours.
- **Expertise and preparation:** people listen to someone who has done the homework.
- **Shared incentives:** show how the goal helps them, not only you.
- **The sponsor's backing:** a signed charter or an executive's visible support, used sparingly.

When you need something from a colleague's manager, ask directly and explain the benefit, and agree on the trade-offs openly. Pressure may work once, but it spends trust that you will need later.

## Async {#async}
Async is short for asynchronous. It means communicating without needing everyone to be present at the same moment, for example through written updates, recorded demos and shared documents in place of live meetings. A reply may come hours later, and that is the expected rhythm.

Why does this matter? For a distributed team, a live meeting at 9 a.m. in one city may fall at midnight in another. Async work lets people contribute in their own working hours and think before they reply. Its costs are slower back-and-forth and a risk that messages are misread.

These habits make it work:
- Write messages that stand alone, with the context, the question and a deadline.
- Put decisions and their reasons in a written place that everyone can find.
- Agree a response time, such as within one working day.
- Keep a few overlapping hours for live conversation about sensitive or complex issues.
- Prefer a short recorded video over a long meeting for demos.

## A worked example {#example}
A project manager, Noor, leads a team with members in Toronto, Berlin and Manila. She has no authority over any of them, since they report to different managers. She needs a design decision by Friday. Let's see what she does.

1. **Clarity.** She posts a message that stands alone: "Decision needed by Friday 12:00 Toronto time: which of the two layouts to use. Context, the options with pictures and the criteria are below. Please reply with your choice and one reason."
2. **Trust and reciprocity.** She starts by thanking Berlin for last week's review and offers to take a task off Manila's list this week.
3. **Async.** She sets a 24-hour reply window. Toronto, Berlin and Manila answer in their own hours.
4. **Decision.** On Friday she records the decision and the reasons in the shared document and posts a link.

Look at the time zones. 9 a.m. in Toronto is about 3 p.m. in Berlin and about 9 or 10 p.m. in Manila, depending on daylight saving time. There is no hour when all three are at a normal work time, so a live meeting would force one person to work late. The async process cost nobody a late night and produced a documented decision.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is trying to lead by escalating to a manager as soon as someone is slow. It damages the relationship and shows that you have no other tools. Ask, explain and help first.

In remote work, a second slip is moving everything into long meetings, which leaves out the people in the least convenient time zones.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Leading without authority relies on clarity, trust, reciprocity and shared goals.
- Async communication lets people contribute across time zones.
- Write messages that stand alone, record decisions and agree on reply times.
- Keep live meetings for the topics that really need them.
